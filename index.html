Multi-Vector Risk in Georgia

Species distribution modelling, social vulnerability and integrated risk assessment

Pshea‑Smith et al. — University of Florida, WRAIR‑EME and USAMRIID

Project Overview

This project models the habitat suitability of sixteen mosquito and tick species of public health relevance across the Republic of Georgia (Caucasus region). Species-level predictions are combined into weighted exposure indices for eight vector groups, then integrated with a census-derived social vulnerability index to produce municipality-level risk estimates following an INFORM-parallel framework.

Outputs are summarised at the scale of Georgia's sixty-four administrative municipalities, with interactive maps and supporting data hosted alongside this page.

Key finding: Risk was highest across the southeastern and western lowlands, with Sagarejo, Qeda and Dedofliswyaro ranking as the top three at-risk municipalities. Tbilisi, despite showing the highest vector-driven hazard score in the country, recorded the third-lowest composite risk owing to its low social vulnerability, illustrating the value of jointly interpreting hazard and vulnerability rather than either component alone.

Key Results

Species distribution models

16 mosquito and tick species modelled across 100 pseudo-absence iterations (BRTs) or 3 evaluation runs (ESMs)
0.64–1.00 cross-validated AUC range across species; all models performed better than random
8 vector groups assessed, from Lyme borreliosis to emerging arboviruses
64 municipalities evaluated for integrated risk, excluding autonomous regions with no census data

Risk index

Risk was concentrated across the southeastern and western sections of the country. The three highest-risk municipalities at baseline were Sagarejo (risk = 0.43; hazard = 0.37, SVI = 0.51), Qeda (0.43; 0.26, 0.70) and Dedofliswyaro (0.42; 0.31, 0.57). The lowest risk values were recorded in Yazbegi and Kutaisi (both 0.10), driven by low hazard and low social vulnerability respectively. Tbilisi recorded the third-lowest risk (0.12) despite showing the highest hazard score nationally (0.86), owing to its low social vulnerability (0.01).

Hazard indices

Across the eight vector groups, municipality-level hazard standard deviations ranged narrowly from 1.16 (Plasmodium vectors) to 1.56 (Aedes-borne viruses). Mean hazard was highest for tick-borne encephalitis virus vectors (3.01, SD = 1.27) and lowest for Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever virus vectors (2.13, SD = 1.44).

Social vulnerability

The social vulnerability index (SVI) had a mean of −0.02 (SD = 0.44) across municipalities. Tbilisi showed the lowest social vulnerability (−1.19), followed by Kutaisi (−1.13) and Batumi (−0.99). The highest SVI values occurred in Dusheti (1.48), Shuakhevi (1.05) and Khulo (0.69), reflecting relatively rural, less-served populations.

Vector Groups

The sixteen modelled species were assigned to eight disease groups based on published vector competence evidence, regional seroprevalence data and molecular detection rates. Contribution weights were derived from the literature and tested via sensitivity analysis, with minimal sensitivity to plausible weight variation.

Modelling Approach

Species distribution models were constructed using a sample-size-scaled framework. Species with sufficient presence records were modelled using boosted regression trees (BRTs) via dismo::gbm.step, with 100 pseudo-absence iterations per species and month-matched covariate extraction via dynamicSDM. Species with fewer than fifty unique presence records were modelled using ensembles of small models (ESMs) via ecospat and biomod2, following Breiner and colleagues (2015, 2018).

Predictor sets were taxon-specific: mosquito models drew on bioclimatic, topographic, land-cover and wind-related covariates; tick models substituted soil variables for wind speed. Final predictor lists were selected by variance inflation factor screening at the taxon level to support cross-species comparability.

Vector exposure indices were constructed using continuous habitat suitability surfaces (E = Σ wi × suiti), with uncertainty propagated via 1,000 Monte Carlo iterations sampled from truncated normal distributions on the species-level weights. Social vulnerability was computed from six Georgian census indicators. Hazard and vulnerability components were combined using an epsilon-adjusted geometric mean formulation to prevent zero values on one axis from nullifying signal on the other.

Data Sources

  • Vector occurrences: VectorMap, the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) and a previously published regional tick dataset. All records were filtered to unique post-2000 entries.
  • Climate data: CHELSA v2.1 bioclimatic variables.
  • Soil data: SoilGrids (tick covariates only).
  • Land cover: ESA WorldCover (categorical, handled as a factor).
  • Social vulnerability: Georgian National Statistics Office census indicators (regions.geostat.ge), aggregated to six municipality-level composite inputs.
  • Administrative boundaries: GeoJSON municipal boundaries distributed by the Georgian National Statistics Office (64-municipality taxonomy).
  • Analytical stack: R 4.5.1 with terra, sf, exactextractr, dismo, gbm, ecospat, biomod2, dynamicSDM, truncnorm and tidyterra.

Public Health Implications

Multi-taxon descriptions of vector ecology remain uncommon, despite vector surveillance and control routinely being performed across taxa by the same public health personnel. Moreover, integrating mosquito and tick distributions with social vulnerability data provides a more holistic basis for resource allocation than single-species or single-pathogen assessments alone.

The contrast between Tbilisi, which recorded the highest hazard but lowest social vulnerability in the country, and municipalities such as Qeda, where moderate hazard combines with high vulnerability to yield top-tier risk scores, highlights the importance of considering both vector ecology and population-level context when prioritising surveillance and control resources. These outputs provide spatially explicit, municipality-level estimates designed to support prioritisation while transparently communicating the uncertainty associated with data-sparse settings.
