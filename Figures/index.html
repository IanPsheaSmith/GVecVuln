Multi-Vector Disease Risk in Georgia

Species distribution modelling, social vulnerability and risk assessment

Pshea-Smith et al. — University of Florida

Project Overview

This project models the distributions of 18 mosquito and tick species of public health relevance in the country of Georgia (Caucasus region). Species-level suitability predictions are integrated into weighted composite disease exposure indices for eight pathogen–vector groups, then combined with a social vulnerability index to produce municipality-level risk assessments following an INFORM-parallel framework.

Georgia’s geographic complexity—spanning lowland river valleys, humid subtropical coastlines, and high-elevation Caucasus terrain—supports diverse vector communities. Describing these distributions is a prerequisite for evidence-based vector control planning in the region.

Key Results

Species distribution models

16 mosquito and tick species modelled across 100 pseudo-absence iterations
0.84–1.00 mean AUC range across species, indicating high model discrimination
8 pathogen–vector disease groups assessed, from Lyme borreliosis to emerging arboviruses
~69 municipalities evaluated for risk, excluding regions with no census data

Risk index

Risk was highest in the southeastern lowlands, with Gori (4.71 ± 0.23), Sagarejo (4.29 ± 0.59), and Axalcixe (4.11 ± 0.15) ranking as the top three municipalities at baseline. Tbilisi had the lowest social vulnerability in the country, resulting in a low composite risk score (0.71 ± 0.05) despite moderate hazard.

Phenological shifts under warming

Spotted fever rickettsioses vectors exhibited the broadest baseline surveillance window (March–November), while emerging Aedes-borne virus vectors showed the most pronounced phenological shifts, with season onset advancing by approximately 2.8 months under +3 °C of warming.

Pathogen–Vector Groups

Sixteen species were assigned to eight disease groups based on published vector competence evidence, regional seroprevalence data, and molecular detection rates. Contribution weights were derived from the literature and tested via sensitivity analysis.

Modelling Approach

Species distribution models were constructed using a three-tier framework scaled by sample size: standard boosted regression trees (BRTs) for species with >100 presence records, conservatively tuned BRTs for species with 50–100 records (tree complexity = 1, 5-fold CV, bag fraction = 0.5), and ensemble of small models (ESMs) via ecospat/biomod2 for rare species with <50 records. Each BRT tier used 100 pseudo-absence iterations with month-matched covariate extraction via dynamicSDM.

Baseline predictions used CHELSA climatologies. Future projections were computed across three CMIP6 global climate models (GFDL-ESM4, MPI-ESM1, UKESM1) using a delta-change methodology, under SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5 pathways summarised at global warming levels of +1.5 °C, +2 °C, and +3 °C.

Disease exposure indices were constructed using a continuous suitability approach (E = Σ wi × suiti) with uncertainty propagated via 1,000 Monte Carlo iterations. Social vulnerability was computed from six Georgian census indicators and integrated with the hazard score via an epsilon-adjusted geometric mean.

Data Sources

  • Vector occurrences: VectorMap database (accessed March 2025) and bespoke field collections
  • Climate data: CHELSA v2.1 bioclimatic variables (baseline); CHELSA-CMIP6 projections (future)
  • Social vulnerability: Georgian National Statistics Office census data (regions.geostat.ge)
  • Administrative boundaries: geodata::gadm() ADM2 for Georgia
  • Spatial analysis tools: R v4.5.1 with terra, sf, exactextractr, dismo, ecospat, biomod2

Public Health Implications

Multi-taxon descriptions of vector ecology remain uncommon despite vector control being routinely performed across taxa by the same public health personnel. Integrating mosquito and tick distributions with social vulnerability data provides a more holistic basis for resource allocation than single-species or single-pathogen assessments alone.

The projected expansion of suitable habitat under warming, combined with phenological shifts that may extend transmission windows by several months for some disease groups, underscores the importance of forward-looking surveillance planning. These outputs provide spatially explicit, municipality-level estimates designed to support prioritisation while transparently communicating uncertainty.
